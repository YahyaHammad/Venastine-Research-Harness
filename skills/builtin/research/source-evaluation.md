## Source evaluation methodology

### Two axes, and both are needed

A source's worth to a claim is **authority** (is this publisher entitled
to assert this kind of thing) multiplied by **relevance** (does this
passage address the claim at hand). Neither alone is a judgement: a
national statistics office is authoritative and useless if the page you
cited is about something else, and a perfectly on-point paragraph is worth
little if it comes from a content farm.

This is the same shape the harness's own scorer uses, deliberately, so the
prose and the number do not teach different things.

### Rank the publisher by what constrains it

Roughly, in descending order: restricted registries where identity is
verified before publication (`.gov`, `.mil`, `.edu`, `ac.uk`),
intergovernmental bodies, standards bodies, peer-reviewed publishers,
established news, preprint servers, tertiary references such as
encyclopaedias, general commercial sites, blog platforms, question-and-
answer forums, social media, content farms.

Two rules keep that from being applied naively. A preprint is not peer
reviewed, whatever the venue's prestige, and saying "published in" of one
is a factual error. A tertiary reference is a **pointer to a source**, not
a source: follow it and cite what it cites, or say explicitly that you are
relying on the summary.

### The domain is not the argument

`evilgov.com` is not a government and `example.ac` is not a university.
Check what the suffix actually is, not what it resembles. A hosted
subdomain inherits the platform's standing, not its host's.

### Verify the quote against the page

If you attribute a sentence to a source, that sentence should be findable
on the page you cite. Paraphrase drifts across a chain of citations
faster than anyone expects, and a quotation that cannot be located is a
finding about the claim, not a formatting problem.

### Separate what was measured from what was concluded

Papers frequently demonstrate something narrow and conclude something
broad, and citations then propagate the conclusion. When you find that
gap, report it — it is often the most valuable single observation
available about a body of literature.

### Adjust for a stated reason, and only a little

You may move a source up or down from its class — a retraction, a
correction, a known conflict of interest, an author who is the primary
authority on exactly this — but the adjustment must be small and it must
come with the reason written down beside it. An unexplained adjustment is
indistinguishable from a preference.

A retraction is not an adjustment. It floors the source.

### Say the strength at the claim, not in a bibliography

"Reported by a single trade publication, not independently confirmed"
belongs in the sentence making the claim. A reader who has to reconstruct
the evidential weight from a reference list will not do it.
